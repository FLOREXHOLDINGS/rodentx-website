Sealed, in writing.

Bats in Geneva homes

Geneva, in Ontario County at the north end of Seneca Lake, has some of the most bat-friendly housing in the Finger Lakes: Victorian-era homes with decorative trim, tall gables, and wide porches; older brick houses; and lakefront homes and cottages that sit quiet for long stretches. Every one of those features creates entry points. Decorative trim is a system of joints, and joints open. A bat needs a gap about three-eighths of an inch wide — the thickness of a pencil — and a Geneva Victorian that has weathered a century of lake-effect winters has gaps to spare.

The lake does the rest. Seneca Lake and the surrounding wine country give bats heavy summer insect forage, and a seasonal or lightly used lakefront home gives them the one thing a colony wants most: an undisturbed attic. Many Geneva bat problems are discovered in spring, when a cottage or a quiet house is opened and the guano, odor, and staining say the roost was occupied all last season — or is occupied now. The usual species is the big brown bat, faithful to a roost that stays open.

Signs you have bats

  • Guano piled in the attic below a roost point, found when a seasonal home is opened
  • Musty, sharp odor in upstairs rooms or near the attic hatch
  • Staining around gable trim, a chimney joint, or a porch roof transition
  • Rustling or chirping in a wall void at dawn or dusk
  • Bats leaving a single roofline point at dusk, night after night

How bat removal works in Geneva

It runs on the X Standard: Inspect, Remove, Exclude, Monitor. The inspection finds the roost and every active entry — on a Victorian, expect the list to include gable trim, porch transitions, and chimney joints — plus the secondary gaps that would become the next entry if only the main one were closed. For seasonal properties, the inspection can be arranged around opening and closing dates, and the findings are written either way.

Season decides the schedule. From roughly May through mid-August, colonies are in the maternity season, raising young that cannot fly; exclusion then would trap them, so New York bat work is timed around that window. A colony found in a Geneva lake house in June gets inspected, mapped, and prepped — secondary gaps sealed — with one-way exits fitted when the colony can leave whole. A colony found in autumn, which is when many seasonal owners discover one, can usually move straight through the process. Winter findings get their own care, because big brown bats may hibernate inside the building. Before any work begins, we tell you what can be done now, what must wait, and why.

When the window is right, one-way exits empty the roost over a series of evenings. Then every entry is sealed with durable, animal-proof materials — on decorative trim, sealed in a way that respects the woodwork — and the full list is documented in writing. Monitoring follows, because a house that sits empty is a house where a failed seal would go unnoticed.

What we seal

Gable peaks and decorative trim joints, ridge lines, soffit and fascia gaps, chimney flashing, porch-roof transitions, and vent openings. Lakefront exposure weathers seals harder than inland work, so materials and fastening are chosen for it — and every seal is listed in writing.

After the bats are out

A season of guano in a closed-up house is the usual aftermath. After exclusion, cleanout and disinfection of the attic — and insulation replacement where needed — restore the space; see Attic Restoration →. For wineries and farm buildings around Seneca Lake with rodent pressure rather than bats, see Commercial Rodent Control →.

Barns, outbuildings, and winery buildings

Bats around Geneva do not limit themselves to houses. Barns, equipment sheds, and winery outbuildings in the farmland around Seneca Lake offer the same deal as an attic — high, dark, and undisturbed — and a colony in a working building is a problem for the people and products under it. The same exclusion process applies: inspection, timed one-way exits, and sealing, scheduled around the building’s use. If the building problem is rodents rather than bats, that runs on the commercial programme instead — see Commercial Rodent Control →.

Questions
