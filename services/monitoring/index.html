Monitoring.
Proof the seal holds.

The job is not finished when the entry is sealed. We check that the seal holds and the activity stops.

What monitoring is

Monitoring is verification. After removal and exclusion, someone has to establish that the seals are holding and the activity has actually stopped — not assume it. That is the fourth step of the X Standard, and it is what separates a finished job from a hopeful one. Monitoring is scheduled follow-up: the sealed entries are re-checked, the attic or the monitoring points are read for fresh signs, and the result is recorded.

What is checked

  • Every seal from the job’s seal list, checked against the list — intact, undisturbed, holding
  • The attic or affected space, for fresh droppings, new nesting, or new runs
  • The entries that were candidates but not in use — the ones an animal would try next
  • For programmes: the monitoring points on the agreed map, read and logged

A check that finds nothing is still a result, and it is recorded as one. The record is what allows a later question — a new noise, a new tenant, an inspector’s query — to be answered from the file rather than from memory.

Signs to watch

Between checks, the building reports on itself. Noise returning — and its timing — is the first sign. Fresh droppings, a disturbed seal, new gnawing at an entry edge, or odor coming back are the others. We explain the signs plainly at the end of the job, because the person who lives or works in the building hears a change before any schedule catches it. A report from you between checks is part of the system, not an interruption of it. Note the date, the place, and what changed — a dated observation is evidence, and evidence is what the follow-up works from. Vague reports produce vague checks; a note that says the noise returned to the north eave on a specific night sends the check to the right seal first.

The monitoring record

Each check produces a record: the date, the seals and points checked, what was found — including nothing — and any action taken. The record accumulates into the building’s file alongside the survey findings and the seal list. For a homeowner it is the proof the job finished. For a business it is compliance material: a dated, written account of a monitored building, ready for the inspector or auditor who asks. Monitoring is the least visible step of the standard and the one that makes the other steps provable.

If activity returns

Returned activity is diagnosed, not just re-treated. The file shows what was sealed and with what, so a failure can be identified as a failed seal, a new entry, or a different animal — each has a different next step. The findings, the seal, and the follow-up stay documented in writing either way. Nothing about returned activity is handled from memory or by assumption — the file is opened, the building is re-read, and the next step follows from what is actually found.

Questions

Until the activity has stopped and the seals have been checked holding — the cadence for your building is agreed in writing as part of the scope. A commercial programme runs monitoring on its agreed schedule; a residential job runs it as the follow-up step of the standard.
The signs the inspection taught you: new noise and its timing, fresh droppings, new gnawing or disturbance at a sealed entry, and odor returning. Note the day and the place. A dated note makes the follow-up check faster and more useful.
It is the fourth step of the X Standard — the step that verifies the other three. On a one-off job it is the follow-up after exclusion; in a commercial programme it is the recurring schedule itself, with a written report each visit.
