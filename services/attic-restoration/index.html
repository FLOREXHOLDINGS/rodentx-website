Attic Restoration.
Rebuild what they damaged.

Animals damage attics — insulation, clean space, and air quality. Restoration cleans and rebuilds what they damaged, after removal and sealing.

What animals leave behind

An occupied attic accumulates damage in a predictable order. Droppings concentrate where the animals roost or den — a raccoon latrine in one spot, bat guano piled below a roost, rodent droppings along the runs. Insulation is flattened where they travel, torn where they nest, and tunnelled where rodents run. Nesting material, food remains, and odor work into the space. The insulation damage is functional as well as sanitary: compressed and displaced insulation insulates less, and the heating cost of a damaged attic is paid every winter until it is corrected.

The sequence

Restoration is the third step of the standard, and it does not move. First the animals are removed. Then the entries are sealed, so nothing re-enters. Then the attic is restored. Every step is documented in writing, and the restoration scope is set by the inspection findings — what is contaminated, what is damaged, and what can stay.

What restoration includes

  • Cleanouts — droppings, nesting material, and contaminated debris bagged and removed
  • Insulation removal — damaged and contaminated insulation taken out where the findings require it
  • Disinfection — treatment of the affected surfaces after the material is out
  • Insulation installation — new insulation installed to replace what was removed

A note on odor

Homeowners often ask whether the smell can simply be treated. Odor in an occupied attic is not in the air; it is in the material — in the insulation and debris the droppings and urine have soaked. Treating the air leaves the source in place, and the smell returns with the first warm day. That is why the restoration sequence removes the contaminated material first and disinfects the surfaces after. When the material is out, the odor is out with it.

How the scope is set

Restoration is scoped from the inspection findings, attic by attic.

A bat roost over one bay may need that bay’s insulation out and the rest left undisturbed. A season of raccoons can mean the latrine area, the travel routes, and the nesting corner — most of the floor. Rodent work usually concentrates where the runs and nests were found. The findings state which case yours is, with the reasoning, before any restoration is agreed. Where insulation comes out, the replacement is part of the same scope, so the attic is never left uninsulated between stages of a job.

Two things restoration is not: it is not a renovation, and it is not sold by default. It returns the attic to a clean, functional state — the state it was in before the animals. Anything beyond that is a different trade and a different conversation.

What you get in writing

The attic’s condition as found, the areas cleaned, what was removed and what was installed, and the state the attic is left in. The file closes the job: the same document that recorded the findings records the correction.
