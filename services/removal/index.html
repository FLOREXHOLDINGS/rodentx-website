Removal.
Humane. Lawful. Species-correct.

Removal matched to the species, the season, and the building. No shortcuts, no handling promises we can’t keep.

How removal works

Removal starts from the inspection: the species, the entry, and the season decide the method. Bats are excluded through one-way exits fitted at the active entries — the colony leaves at dusk and cannot return. Raccoons are cage-trapped or excluded through one-way devices, depending on the den and whether young are present. Squirrels are trapped or excluded through one-way doors at the entry they are using. Mice and rats are removed as part of a programme — removal, sealing, and monitoring together, because a building’s rodent population is a condition of the building, not a single animal.

The methods are the lawful ones for the species and the situation, and they are matched to the building: a method that works in a detached barn is not automatically the method for an occupied attic. What will be used on your building, and why, is set out in the written scope before work begins.

Timing and young animals

Wildlife work runs on the animals’ calendar. Bat exclusion waits out the maternity season, when the young cannot fly. Raccoon and squirrel work accounts for litters in the structure. These are not delays for their own sake — a removal that leaves flightless young in a wall void has not solved the problem, it has buried it. Where a restriction applies, you are told what can proceed now, what waits, and the date logic behind it, before any work begins.

Why the method follows the animal

The method is chosen from the animal’s behaviour, because each species leaves differently. A bat colony leaves as a group at dusk, which is what makes one-way exits work. A raccoon den is defended by its occupant, which is why trapping and timing matter more than devices. A squirrel runs a daily route through a single entry, which a one-way door at that entry answers. Rodents do none of these things at building scale — they are a population in the structure, which is why rodent removal is a programme rather than an event. Matching the method to the behaviour is the difference between a removal and a disturbance: the wrong method scatters animals into new parts of the building and makes the real job larger.

What removal does not do

Removal alone does not keep animals out. Without sealing, the next animal uses the same entry — raccoons and bats return to known sites by memory, and rodents follow the scent of established runs. That is why every removal leads to exclusion. The seal is the point.

After removal

When the animals are out, the record of the removal goes into the job file: what was removed, from where, and by what method. Then the entries are sealed, contamination is dealt with where restoration applies, and monitoring confirms the building stays quiet. The sequence is the X Standard, and it does not change from job to job: Inspect / Remove / Exclude / Monitor.
