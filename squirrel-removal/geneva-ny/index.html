Squirrel Removal in
Geneva, NY.
Out, and kept out.

Squirrels in Geneva’s historic and lakefront homes chew in through soffits and vents. We remove them, seal every entry with chew-proof materials, and document the work.

Squirrels in Geneva attics

Geneva’s housing is a squirrel’s opportunity map: Victorian-era homes near Hobart and William Smith Colleges with decorative trim, tall gables, and porch roofs meeting main roofs at a dozen angles; older homes under mature canopy on the streets climbing from Seneca Lake; and lakefront houses and cottages that sit empty for stretches of the year. Gray squirrels work all of it. They need one soft soffit edge or one loose gable vent, and the chewing that follows is quick — a hole in days, a nest in the attic within the week.

Seasonal homes carry a particular risk. A squirrel that moves into a quiet Geneva lake house in October has an undisturbed attic until spring, and by the time the owners open the house, there may be a winter’s worth of nesting, droppings, and chewed insulation — sometimes two litters’ worth of damage. Year-round homes get the standard pattern instead: a litter in late winter, another in late summer, and daytime noise over the bedrooms in between.

Signs squirrels are in

  • Daytime running, scratching, or rolling sounds in the attic — mornings are loudest
  • A chewed hole at a fascia corner, soffit, or gable vent, sometimes with trim shavings on the ground
  • Leaf-and-insulation nests in the attic, found when a seasonal home is opened
  • Chewed insulation or wiring in the attic
  • The same squirrel, on the same roof corner, every day

How squirrel removal works in Geneva

The work runs Inspect, Remove, Exclude, Monitor. Inspection establishes the entries — primary, secondary, and the ones being chewed but not yet through — the state of the attic, and whether young are present. On Geneva’s Victorians, the entry survey pays attention to trim joints and porch transitions; on lakefront homes, to the roofline sections that face the trees the squirrels commute from. Damage is documented as found, wiring first.

Removal is matched to the entry and the season: one-way exit doors where they suit the situation, lawful trapping where they do not, and a sequence that accounts for litters — young squirrels stay in the nest for their first weeks, so removal is timed and ordered so the family leaves together. For seasonal properties, the sequence can be planned around opening and closing dates, with the findings in writing either way.

Sealing is the step that ends the cycle. Entries are closed with durable, animal-proof materials — metal-backed where squirrels will test them — and on decorative trim the work is fitted to the woodwork. Every seal is listed in writing. Monitoring follows, which matters most for houses that will sit empty again: you will know what to check at closing, and what a failed sign looks like.

What we seal

Fascia and soffit entries, gable vents (re-screened and backed), porch and addition roof transitions, and ridge and roofline gaps. Lakefront weather tests seals harder, so materials are chosen and fastened for exposure — and documented, entry by entry.

For seasonal homes: a closing routine

The cheapest squirrel work is the inspection that happens before you close the house. Our findings will list the soft points — the soffit starting to rot, the vent screen lifting — that become next winter’s entry. Sealing those on the closing visit costs a fraction of a spring cleanout, and it is the difference between opening a quiet house and opening a nursery.

Chimneys and vents

Two Geneva entry points deserve their own mention. Chimneys — especially the older, wide-flue chimneys on Victorian homes — are used as roosting and entry shafts, and a standard cap that is not animal-proof is an invitation. Roof and gable vents are the other: the factory screen behind a vent is light material, and squirrels chew through it in an afternoon. Where inspection finds them, both are sealed and screened with materials that hold — and they are on the written seal list like everything else. Ask about both at inspection if your home still has its original vents.

Questions

Leave the attic as it is and request an inspection. Do not block the entry yourself — if squirrels or young are inside, a blocked entry forces them into wall voids or seals them in. The inspection establishes what is in residence and the right sequence to empty and seal the attic.
Because it worked. Squirrels return to known entries and known routes, and they test the same construction details — soffit corners, vent edges — on every house. Sealing every entry found, with materials they cannot chew, is what breaks the habit.
If squirrels are active inside, waiting usually means more chewing — wiring included — and possibly another litter. The inspection will tell you honestly whether the situation can wait or should not.
