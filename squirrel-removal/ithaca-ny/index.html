Squirrels in Ithaca attics

Ithaca is built into wooded hillsides, and gray squirrels run the canopy over most of its older neighborhoods — Fall Creek, Cayuga Heights, the streets climbing East and South Hill. The housing matches the terrain: older homes with layered additions, dormers, and porches, roofs that step down slopes, and a large stock of rentals near Cornell University and Ithaca College where maintenance is deferred and soffits go soft unnoticed. For a squirrel, that is a menu. It travels the branches, tests the fascia corners, and chews through the first rotten edge it finds — then the attic becomes a nursery in late winter and again in late summer.

Rental housing raises the stakes. A squirrel family over a bedroom ceiling is a habitability complaint within days, and the entry that one tenant reports in March is the entry that was soft the previous October. Owners and property managers across Ithaca deal with the same cycle: the buildings that stay sealed stay quiet, and the buildings that get a patch get a repeat.

Signs squirrels are in

  • Daytime running, scratching, and rolling in the attic or ceiling — heaviest in the morning
  • A chewed opening at a soffit, fascia corner, or gable vent, with shavings below
  • Nests of leaves and shredded insulation in the attic
  • Tenants reporting noise in top-floor rooms, in more than one unit
  • Squirrels commuting along the same branch-to-roof route every day

How squirrel removal works in Ithaca

Inspect, Remove, Exclude, Monitor — the X Standard, applied to a roofline that is rarely simple. The inspection maps the active entry, the secondary entries, and the condition of the soffits and vents across the building; on Ithaca’s stepped, hillside roofs that survey is the difference between solving the problem and relocating it one dormer over. In the attic we document nesting, young if present, and damage — wiring above all, because squirrels chew constantly and older Ithaca wiring runs were never routed with squirrels in mind.

Removal follows the findings: one-way exit doors where they fit the entry and the situation, lawful trapping where they do not. If a litter is in the nest, the sequence is set so the family leaves whole — sealing young inside is both inhumane and, practically, a dead-animal problem inside a wall within days. For rentals, the scope, the sequence, and the access plan are put in writing for the owner or manager before work starts, so tenants get dates instead of surprises.

Only when the attic is empty do the entries get sealed — with durable, animal-proof materials, metal-backed at chew points — and the list of seals is documented. Monitoring follows, and for multi-unit buildings the monitoring can roll into a documented programme alongside rodent work; see Commercial Programs →.

What we seal

Soffit and fascia entries, gable and roof vents, dormer and porch-roof transitions, and roofline gaps at additions. Ithaca’s older roofs usually have more than one candidate entry; all of them found on inspection are sealed and listed in writing.

The rental cycle, broken

Most repeat squirrel calls in Ithaca trace back to the same soft soffit or unscreened vent, season after season. The inspection findings include the weak points that are not entries yet. Owners who seal those on the same visit-cycle — instead of after the next litter — are the ones whose buildings drop off the squirrel map.

For tenants: what to write down

If you rent, the fastest route to a fix is a clear record: when the noise happens (squirrels keep daytime hours), which room it is over, and any entry you can see from the ground — a chewed corner, a lifted vent screen. Send that to your landlord or manager. It turns "there is a noise" into an inspection request with the species half-identified, and the written findings from that inspection give the owner the scope and the sequence in one document. RodentX scopes the work for the owner, and the sealing list becomes part of the building’s maintenance record — which is how the repeat calls stop.
