Squirrels in Auburn attics

Auburn’s older neighborhoods are gray squirrel habitat with houses in it: mature street trees, canopy over the roofs, and a housing stock — much of it built before 1940 — with wood soffits, fascia, and trim that squirrels have been testing for decades. A squirrel does not need a hole. It needs a soft edge: a piece of fascia the paint failed on, a soffit joint that rotted, a gable vent with a loose screen. From a branch overhanging the roof, it finds that edge and chews its own doorway, and once a female decides an Auburn attic is a nursery, she will defend the route back to it.

The pattern is seasonal. Squirrels raise litters in late winter into spring and again in late summer, and attics are chosen for exactly those months — warm, dry, and safe from predators. The rest of the year the same entries get used for shelter and food storage runs. An entry chewed this year will be reused next year, by the same squirrel or the next one, until it is sealed with something a squirrel cannot chew through.

Signs squirrels are in

  • Running, scratching, or rolling sounds in the attic — in the daytime, especially morning and late afternoon
  • A chewed hole at a fascia corner, soffit, or gable vent, often with grey fur or chew shavings below it
  • Nesting material — leaves and shredded insulation — in the attic
  • Droppings and acorn or nut debris in the attic or eaves
  • Squirrels on the roof at the same corner, day after day

How squirrel removal works in Auburn

The X Standard applies: Inspect, Remove, Exclude, Monitor. Inspection finds the active entry and the secondary ones — squirrels often maintain a backup — and checks the attic for nesting, young, and damage, including the one that matters most: wiring. Squirrels chew constantly to maintain their teeth, and insulation and electrical runs in an older Auburn attic are at real risk; the inspection documents what we find, plainly.

Removal is matched to what the inspection found. Where a one-way exit door fits the situation, squirrels leave and cannot re-enter; where trapping is the right method, it is done lawfully and checked on schedule. Timing is part of the method: if young are in the nest, the removal is sequenced so the family leaves together rather than sealing pups inside — a sealed-in litter is a worse problem than the one you called about. Once the attic is confirmed empty, the entries are sealed, and only then.

Sealing a squirrel entry is not caulk and hope. Chewed wood entries are closed with durable, animal-proof material — metal-backed where the chewing pressure is — and the repair list covers every entry found, primary and secondary, documented in writing. Then we monitor: you will know the signs to watch, and what happens if activity returns.

What we seal

Fascia and soffit entries, gable vents (re-screened and backed), roofline gaps, and the transitions at porches and additions that older Auburn homes are full of. Entries are sealed with materials chosen for a chewing animal, and every seal is listed in writing — what was sealed, and with what.

Keeping them out of an older Auburn home

The long game is the roofline itself: sound fascia, screened vents, and trim that is not soft. Our inspection findings will name the weak points we did not seal because they were not entries yet — the spots a squirrel will test next season. Homeowners who work through that list, in order, are the ones who do not call twice.

Branches are bridges

Most Auburn squirrel entries start with a commute: a branch within jumping distance of the roof. Trimming limbs back from the roofline does not seal anything, but it removes the easy route and makes the sealed entries the hard way in — worth doing alongside the sealing, especially on streets where the canopy touches every roof. The inspection findings will point out which trees are doing the bridging on your house.
