Bat Removal in
Auburn, NY.
Exclusion-first.

Bats in Auburn’s older attics are removed by exclusion — one-way exits, sealed entries, and timing set by New York’s maternity season, not by shortcuts.

Bats in Auburn homes

Auburn is the Cayuga County seat, sitting at the north end of Owasco Lake — and a large share of its housing is older: nineteenth- and early-twentieth-century homes with layered rooflines, brick chimneys, wood soffits and fascia, and additions built onto additions. That construction style is exactly what attic bats look for. A gap where a porch roof meets the main roof, a shrunken piece of trim at a gable peak, a chimney flashing joint that opened up over decades — bats need an opening only about three-eighths of an inch wide, roughly the thickness of a pencil, and an older Auburn roofline offers dozens of candidates.

The lake and the Owasco Outlet give bats strong summer feeding nearby, and the mature street trees through Auburn’s older neighborhoods complete the picture: food, water, and quiet attic roosts within a short flight. The species most often found in Finger Lakes attics is the big brown bat — a colony-forming bat that returns to the same roost year after year if the entries stay open.

Signs you have bats

  • Droppings (guano) collecting in the attic, often in a pile below one roost point
  • A sharp, musty odor near the attic or an upstairs wall
  • Brown staining or grease marks around a roofline gap, vent, or chimney joint
  • Chirping, scratching, or rustling in a wall void or the attic at dawn or dusk
  • Bats seen leaving the roofline at dusk — count the exit point, not just the bats

How bat removal works in Auburn

Bat removal is exclusion, done in the right order and the right season. It starts with an inspection: we identify the roost, find every entry the colony is using — on older Auburn homes there is usually more than one — and map the secondary gaps that have to be sealed so the colony cannot simply move to the next opening.

The timing matters, and it is set by New York’s bats, not by preference. During the maternity season — roughly May through mid-August — female bats are raising young that cannot fly yet. Excluding a colony in that window traps flightless pups inside, which is why reputable bat work in New York is scheduled around it: inspection and sealing of secondary gaps can be planned, but the one-way exits that empty a roost are installed when the whole colony can leave on its own. Winter changes the picture again, because big brown bats may hibernate inside the structure. We will tell you plainly what can be done now, what has to wait for the legal window, and why — before any work begins.

When the window is right, one-way exclusion devices go on the active entries: bats leave at dusk and cannot get back in. Once the roost is confirmed empty, the entries are sealed permanently and the work is documented. Then we monitor — because an older Auburn roofline deserves a follow-up check, not a promise and a wave.

What we seal

Gable peaks and ridge lines, soffit and fascia joints, chimney flashing gaps, roof-to-wall transitions at porches and additions, and vent openings — sealed with durable, animal-proof materials. On a century-old Auburn home, sealing is carpentry-grade work: the material has to match a roofline that is not square anymore. Every seal is listed in writing — what was sealed, and with what.

After the bats are out

A colony that has used an attic leaves guano and odor behind. Once the bats are out and the entries are sealed, cleanout and disinfection of the affected area — and insulation work where it is needed — can follow. That sequence matters: restoration comes after exclusion, never before. See Attic Restoration → for how that work runs.

Why Auburn colonies come back to the same house

Big brown bats navigate by memory and return to a roost that worked. That is why a colony "removed" without sealing is a colony again next summer — and why the seal list, not the exit count, is the measure of the job.
