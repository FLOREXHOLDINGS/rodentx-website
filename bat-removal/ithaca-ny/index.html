Bats in Ithaca homes

Ithaca sits at the south end of Cayuga Lake in Tompkins County, built into steep, wooded hillsides — and its housing stock is a bat’s shortlist. Older homes in neighborhoods like Fall Creek and Cayuga Heights, the dense rental stock around Cornell University and Ithaca College, and generations of additions, dormers, and rebuilt porches all produce the same thing: complicated rooflines full of small gaps. Bats need an opening about three-eighths of an inch wide. An older Ithaca house, settled into a hillside and repaired in layers for a hundred years, offers plenty.

The setting feeds the problem. The gorges, the lake, and heavy tree cover give bats insects and water all summer, and quiet attics — especially in rental homes empty between terms and in houses where the attic is rarely opened — give them undisturbed roosts. The species most often involved in Finger Lakes attics is the big brown bat, which returns to the same roost season after season when the entries stay open. In a multi-unit or rental building, one colony in the roof can mean noise and odor complaints in several units at once.

Signs you have bats

  • Guano in the attic, usually piled below a single roost point
  • Odor or staining near the attic hatch, a chimney, or an upstairs wall
  • Chirping or rustling in wall voids at dawn or dusk
  • Bats leaving one roofline point at dusk, evening after evening
  • Tenants reporting sounds or smell in top-floor rooms of a rental

How bat removal works in Ithaca

The process is Inspect, Remove, Exclude, Monitor — in that order. Inspection comes first: we find the roost, the active entries, and the secondary gaps across the whole roofline, including the porch and addition transitions that older Ithaca homes accumulate. In a rental or multi-unit building, the inspection also establishes which units are affected and what access is needed, so owners and property managers get the scope in writing before anything is scheduled.

Timing is set by the bats’ calendar. Through the maternity season — roughly May through mid-August — colonies are raising young that cannot fly, and exclusion in that window would trap them inside; New York bat work is therefore scheduled around it. That timing question lands hard in Ithaca, where the discovery often happens during summer turnover between student leases. The honest answer is the same: inspect and map now, seal the secondary gaps, and install one-way exits when the whole colony can leave. Winter discoveries need care too, since big brown bats may be hibernating in the structure. We explain what can be done now and what has to wait, before work begins.

At the right time, one-way exits go on the active entries. The colony leaves at dusk and cannot re-enter. Once the roost is empty, every entry is sealed with durable, animal-proof materials and listed in writing, and we monitor to confirm the building stays quiet.

What we seal

Ridge and gable gaps, soffit and fascia joints, chimney and flashing openings, roof-to-wall transitions at dormers, porches, and additions, and vent penetrations. On hillside homes with steep pitches and layered rooflines, the seal list is usually longer than the homeowner expects — which is exactly why it is written down, entry by entry.

After the bats are out

Guano accumulation and contaminated insulation are the usual leftovers, and in a rental they are the part tenants can still smell after the bats are gone. Cleanout, disinfection, and insulation work follow exclusion — see Attic Restoration →. For landlords and managers running several buildings, rodent and wildlife monitoring can be folded into a documented programme; see Commercial Programs →.

What an Ithaca bat inspection covers

Steep roofs and layered additions make Ithaca inspections a ground-and-attic job as much as a ladder job. We check the roofline from outside — including the porch and dormer transitions that are hard to see from the street — then the attic, where the guano pattern tells us where the roost is and how established it is. In multi-unit buildings we also establish which entries serve which part of the roof, so the written scope matches the building instead of a guess. You get the entry list, the timing, and the sequence — before anyone books the work.
